# Plain Writing

Write for the reader who will receive the finished text. Follow the requested language, register, genre, and scope of revision. For a draft in progress, preserve the author's voice and keep the parts the user asked to retain.

## Start with the assignment

Identify the audience, the outcome they need, and the strongest point the available material supports. For a line edit, fix the requested passage at the requested scale. For a new draft, decide what the reader needs in the opening to make sense of the rest.

Collect the facts that carry the argument. Keep names, dates, numbers, units, quotations, and citations attached to their claims. Use terms the audience and sources recognize. Explain unfamiliar terms through their role in the current example.

## Everyday writing

- In a message or email, put the decision, request, or status near the top. Give the relevant owner, deadline, and next action where the reader can find them.
- In a report or explanation, lead with the result or question. Use a specific example when a reader needs to understand how something works. Name the person or system doing the work and use a verb that describes what happened.
- Replace generic praise, apologies, or scene-setting with the detail that earns the sentence. Keep wording that sounds like the author. Use headings and lists when they help readers find or compare information.

## Academic writing

- In an abstract or introduction, establish the problem and the specific gap. State the contribution in concrete terms and put the strongest relevant result nearby. Group the evidence around what the paper shows.
- In methods and results, keep established terminology, exact definitions, relevant baselines, units, sample sizes, uncertainty, and experimental conditions. Keep a figure, table, or citation next to the claim it supports. Preserve LaTeX commands, labels, and citation keys when editing source files.
- Match verbs to the study design and evidence. Use causal wording when the design supports it. Distinguish the measured result, your interpretation, and an implication for future work. State material limitations and trade-offs in concrete terms.
- In a reviewer reply, answer the concern directly. Point to the new evidence or the revised passage when the author has supplied it. Keep requests for missing analyses separate from the copy.

Use the [worked examples](references/examples.md) when they help show the appropriate level of revision.

## Finish and check

Make routine choices about order and phrasing, then finish the supported draft. If an unknown detail changes what the reader should believe or do, leave it for verification and name the exact gap after the draft. Ask a focused question if that gap prevents a useful draft.

Read the finished text as someone who has never seen the notes. Resolve unclear references, remove repeated setup, and check that terms, measurements, attributions, and claim boundaries survived the edit. Deliver usable copy in the requested format. Include a change summary when asked.
